Regulation of cognition
Lower knowledge of cognitionYou do not report a clear picture of how you learn — which strategies you use, when they work, what you are good and bad at. That is not a deficit of ability; it is a map nobody has asked you to draw. Chapter 8's calibration finding applies: people who cannot see their own strategies tend to over-estimate how well they understood.
Moderate knowledge of cognitionYou have some picture of how you learn. Notice which items you rated low — when a strategy works (conditional knowledge) is usually the gap, and it is the one that matters most for choosing what to do with a hard chapter.
Higher knowledge of cognitionYou report a clear picture of your own strategies and when they work. Two cautions: this is a self-report, and knowing about strategies is not the same as using them — read your regulation bar next.
Lower regulation of cognitionYou do not report much planning, monitoring or checking while you learn. This is the profile that reads a chapter, feels it made sense, and finds out on the test — Priya's profile, and most people's. Every item on this scale is a behaviour, and behaviours are learnable one at a time.
Moderate regulation of cognitionYou plan, monitor or check some of the time. Look at which phase is lowest — before (planning), during (monitoring), or after (evaluation). Most students are weakest after: the task ends and nobody asks what was learned.
Higher regulation of cognitionYou report actively planning, monitoring and evaluating your learning. This is the profile that catches "it made sense" before the test does. Worth asking: did you learn it, or were you taught it? Most people who have it were taught it explicitly — which is the argument for teaching it.

Read the two bars against each other.

Knowledge of cognition is what you know about how you learn. Regulation is what you do. The common pattern is a higher first bar than second: people can describe good strategies they do not use. If that is your pattern, you have found the difference between a definition and a schema, on yourself, in the week Priya found it on a celery stick.

No reference line, and a shortened scale.

The full MAI is 52 items, answered true/false. These twenty are drawn from it across both factors and all eight sub-processes, answered on a five-point scale so the bars have something to show — a common classroom adaptation, not a validated short form. Published norms are for the 52-item version and would not transfer. Treat the bars as a structured self-observation.

Notes

Calibration is the concept. Chapter 8's finding is that learners misjudge what they know — and that they misjudge most after fluent, easy study: rereading, a clear explanation, a good summary from a tool. Your regulation bar is, in effect, a measure of how often you check. The lower it is, the more your sense of understanding is running on feel.

Priya's bars. Guess them. Knowledge of cognition: probably moderate — she knows note-taking works for her. Regulation: the monitoring items — I ask myself questions about how well I am doing — are the ones she has never had a reason to do, because every check she met came back green. Your Synthesis this week asks for one move in your subject; one option is a monitoring prompt built into the task, so the learner does not need the trait.

What this cannot tell you. Self-report of metacognition has a well-known problem: the people least able to monitor their learning are also least able to report accurately on whether they monitor it. Your score is a claim you are making about yourself. The test of it is the next retrieval check — compare your confidence rating to your score.

Source. Schraw, G., & Dennison, R. S. (1994). Assessing metacognitive awareness. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 19(4), 460–475. The full inventory is on Canvas if you want the complete picture.
