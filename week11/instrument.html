The motivation scales of the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire, the MSLQ (Pintrich et al., 1991). Six scales: why you work in a course, how much it is worth to you, whether you think you can do it, and how tests feel. Scored in your browser. Lower: intrinsic goalsYou take on course work mostly for other reasons than the challenge. That is common, and it is worth knowing which reasons. A task that only offers challenge will not pull you much.
Moderate: intrinsic goalsChallenge and curiosity are part of why you work in this course, alongside other reasons. Most students land here.
Higher: intrinsic goalsYou work in this course for the challenge and the understanding. Remember the drawing study: an expected reward for something you already enjoy can crowd this out.
Lower: extrinsic goalsGrades and comparison with others don't drive you much in this course. Points and rankings would move you less than their designer expects.
Moderate: extrinsic goalsGrades matter to you, about as much as to most students.
Higher: extrinsic goalsGrades, rank and showing your ability to others drive a lot of your effort. That gets work done. It is also the profile most at risk of asking Mila's question: which option is worth more?
Lower: task valueThis course isn't worth much to you right now: not very interesting, important or useful. With value low, expectancy can't carry the effort alone. Which kind of value is missing?
Moderate: task valueYou see a fair amount of value in this course. Most students land here.
Higher: task valueYou find this course interesting, important or useful, and probably more than one of the three. Value is doing a lot of your motivating.
Lower: control of learningYou are not sure your own effort decides how much you learn here. That leans toward stable or outside attributions. Ask what you would blame a low grade on.
Moderate: control of learningYou mostly believe your effort and study methods decide what you learn. Most students land here.
Higher: control of learningYou believe your effort decides the outcome: an internal, controllable attribution. That is the explanation that keeps people trying after a failure.
Lower: self-efficacyYour confidence that you can do well in this course is lower than most students'. Remember that self-efficacy is about this task, and that its strongest source is a mastery experience: one real success you earned.
Moderate: self-efficacyYou are fairly confident you can do the work here. Most students land here.
Higher: self-efficacyYou are confident you can master this course. High self-efficacy predicts choosing challenges and persisting. Check it against your grades so far: calibration matters too.
Lower: test anxietyTests don't worry you much. Some of your future students will feel the opposite, and their worry will crowd their working memory.
Moderate: test anxietyTests worry you somewhat, at about the level of most students.
Higher: test anxietyTests bring worry and a racing heart. Both parts of anxiety, the thoughts and the body, can crowd working memory, so you may know more than your score shows. Your body's signals are also a source of self-efficacy: they can read as "I'm not ready" when you are.

Read these as information about you. A high extrinsic score is not a flaw, and a high anxiety score does not mean you will do badly. Read the six together: expectancy (self-efficacy, control of learning) and value (intrinsic goals, task value) multiply. Teachers tend to motivate students the way they are motivated themselves, so your profile also predicts the motivators you might over-use.

Notes

Why this scale, this week. Chapter 10 describes four views of motivation. The MSLQ measures the cognitive view on you: value (intrinsic goals, extrinsic goals, task value), expectancy (control of learning beliefs, self-efficacy), and emotion (test anxiety). Extrinsic goals sit next to the drawing study. Control of learning is an attribution: does your effort decide the outcome? Self-efficacy is Michael's question about himself.

What this cannot tell you. These are self-reports: they describe how you see yourself, which is not always how you act. The reference lines come from 380 Midwestern college students in 1990; the manual's authors say the MSLQ has no norms and should be compared within a course. It measures nothing about the sociocultural view: the community you work in is not on this page.
