Part 0 — Settle it, or agree to disagree?

"Let's agree to disagree" ends an argument politely. Sometimes that's honest, and sometimes it stops the argument just before the evidence arrives. Sort six claims, then check your sort against the key.

1 · "Memorizing the stages of mitosis is a critical thinking task."

Look at what the task asks the learner's mind to do. If the answer can be recalled, it is a memory task, however serious the topic. Ask students to predict what happens to a cell when one stage fails, and it becomes thinking.

2 · "Students learn best when teaching matches their learning style."

It has been tested many times. Matching teaching to a preferred style does not improve learning (Pashler et al., 2008). People keep believing it because pictures and sound help everyone, which feels like proof.

3 · "Rereading your notes is a good way to study."

Testing yourself beats rereading in study after study; that is the retrieval and generation work from Week 6. Rereading feels productive because the page gets familiar, and familiar feels like learned.

4 · "A student who can define a term understands it."

Priya defined osmosis correctly and could not explain the limp celery (Week 7). A definition is evidence of memory. Understanding shows when the learner uses the idea on a case they have not seen.

5 · "Learning a second language matters more than learning to code."

This ranks two good things, and the ranking depends on what you want school to do. Evidence can tell you what each one builds; it cannot choose for you. Agreeing to disagree is honest here, as long as each side says what it values.

6 · "Homework should be dropped in elementary school."

Part of it is a question of evidence: what homework does for achievement in the early grades (on average, very little). Part of it is values: family time, habits, fairness. Separate the two before you argue, or you will argue past each other.

On a B, "agree to disagree" is honest. On an A, it is a way to stop before the evidence arrives.

A serious topic can hide a memory task. Judge the task by what the learner's mind has to do. You'll build the version that needs thinking in Part 4.

Part 2 — Three students, three lenses

Reread the case. For each student: the behaviour, the term for it, and what it says about the task — not the student.

Which bridge did he fail? What in the homework made it unnecessary until Friday?

Which system produced "they cancel"? Was he wrong to feel finished?

Which half of creativity was she doing — and which half had no room in the task?

Not a diagnosis of the students — every lens points at the task's design.
