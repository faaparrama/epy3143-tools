Interest (I-type)
Deprivation (D-type)
Lower interest curiosityYou do not chase novelty for its own sake. That is not a deficit — but notice that a course structured around exploration will feel like work rather than play, and you may need to build your own reasons to go further than required.
Moderate interest curiosityYou explore when something catches you. The practical question is what makes something catch you — because that is the lever you will be pulling on your own students.
Higher interest curiosityExploring is its own reward for you. Watch for the teaching trap: it is easy to design lessons that would have worked on you, and most of your students will not share this score.
Lower deprivation curiosityNot knowing sits comfortably with you. Piaget would say disequilibrium does not press on you hard — which makes it easier to leave a confusion unresolved. In Chapter 2's terms, the cheap exit is rejection, and it will not feel like giving up.
Moderate deprivation curiosityUnresolved questions bother you somewhat. You will push on a confusion if it matters, and let it go if it does not — which is a reasonable strategy and worth being deliberate about.
Higher deprivation curiosityNot knowing genuinely itches. This is disequilibrium as a trait, and it is the engine Piaget describes — you resolve confusions rather than parking them. The cost is that it is hard to stop, and not every question deserves the hours.

Low agreement with fixed statementsYou largely reject the idea that intelligence is set. Check this against your growth score below — if both are low, you may hold a domain-specific view rather than a general one.
MixedYou partly agree that intelligence is fixed. Most people land here, and most who land here are thinking of a specific subject while they answer. Which one were you picturing?
High agreement with fixed statementsYou lean toward intelligence being stable. Worth sitting with: this is the belief that makes a struggling student look like evidence of a limit rather than a student mid-accommodation.
Low agreement with growth statementsYou are sceptical that intelligence changes much. That is a defensible read of the evidence on some constructs — and it will shape how long you persist with a student who is not moving.
MixedYou hold growth beliefs conditionally. Naming the condition is the useful move: change in what, by how much, over what time?
High agreement with growth statementsYou strongly endorse growth. Now the hard question — you knew which answer a teacher preparation programme wants. So does every student who fills out a survey for you.

Before you write Part 1

Read your D-type score next to your fixed/growth scores. High D-type with a fixed view is the difficult combination: it bothers you not to know, and you believe you cannot change how well you can know. That resolves toward rejection more often than accommodation.

Then the part that is not about you. Curiosity is built by opportunity — museums, books in the house, adults with time to answer questions, summers at a camp in North Carolina. A student who arrives with less of it has usually had fewer chances to develop it, not less capacity for it.

Which is why "teach to students' interests" is only half a strategy. Taught alone, it ratifies the gap.

The job is to nurture and expand curiosity, not merely to discover it.

Look at the roster while you write. D'Andre spends his summers at a talent development camp. Nick has never been offered one. Neither fact is about their capacity.
