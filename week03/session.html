Today's driving question

What would Piaget, Vygotsky, and Bronfenbrenner say about generative AI use in learning?

~10 min · Theory Review ~50 min · Applied Workshop

Jean Piaget

Constructivism & Schema Development

The concept

Humans are active learners who build knowledge by interacting directly with the physical world.

  • Assimilation: new info slots into an existing schema — no real cognitive change.
  • Accommodation: the schema itself must restructure. Requires disequilibrium — the productive struggle that drives real learning.
🔑 Your job: engineer the right confusion, then guide them through it.

→ What this means for AI

No true construction

LLMs don't interact with a physical world. Knowledge is static once training ends — there's no ongoing experience to accommodate.

Disembodied pattern-matching

Where a child builds an internal schema by touching, failing, revising, an LLM maps statistical relationships between tokens. It completes a prediction — it doesn't construct a reality.

Zero disequilibrium by design

AI gives answers immediately. That removes the friction Piaget identified as the engine of deep learning.

Lev Vygotsky

Social Constructivism & ZPD

The concept

Cognition is fundamentally social. Learning happens in the ZPD — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with a More Knowledgeable Other (MKO).

  • Humans learn through associative memory tied to emotion and social context.
  • Scaffolding requires reading the learner — frustration, readiness, body language.
  • True MKOs bring shared meaning and genuine intent to help.

→ What this means for AI

Pattern completion ≠ comprehension

LLMs operate through next-step prediction. They generate the statistically likely continuation — they don't comprehend the learner's intent or struggle.

No intersubjectivity

AI cannot detect emotional shutdown, frustration, or confusion from tone. The shared meaning Vygotsky saw as essential to real scaffolding simply isn't there.

No intent to teach

A human MKO cares whether learning happens. An LLM predicts tokens — it has no stake in the outcome.

Urie Bronfenbrenner

Ecological Systems Theory

The concept

Development happens within nested layers of influence — from direct daily interactions out to broad cultural forces.

Microsystem Direct, face-to-face interactions: family, classroom, peers
Exosystem Settings that affect the child indirectly — they don't participate, but are shaped by them
Macro & Chronosystem Culture, values, and historical timing of major shifts

→ What this means for AI

AI as an exosystem force

Currently, AI functions as an exosystem influence — it shapes student learning without the student participating in its creation or design.

No microsystem anchor

A human learner is embedded in a microsystem: gravity, touch, family, local culture. AI has none of this. Its "knowledge" is purely derivative of human-authored text — it lacks the common sense that comes from living in a physical ecological system.

The workshop question

Could AI ever move from an impersonal exosystem tool closer to a microsystem influence — something more like an MKO — for your learners? That's what you'll examine today.

Applied Task

Open your workbench and work through the four parts below.

1

Choose a concept

Pick something your future students/clients typically struggle to learn because of their development or prior knowledge.

2

Your plan — before AI (2 pts)

Using Piaget's accommodation and Vygotsky's ZPD + scaffolding, describe what you would do to ensure that concept is understood.

3

Try this prompt in any chatbot

"Explain [Your concept] for a student in grade [Your grade] and give me two scaffolds. Don't just simplify vocabulary; actually adjust the type of explanation to match how this age/grade typically thinks."

4

Evaluate (4 pts) & Peer Feedback (4 pts)

Did the AI actually reflect stage-appropriate thinking? Could it function as an MKO or Microsystem? Then check a classmate's response and improve it from a student perspective.

Debrief

AI may reasonably handle

  • Generating grade-differentiated explanations
  • Producing practice sets and worked examples
  • Drafting a first-pass scaffold or rubric

What stays with the teacher

  • Sensing when a learner has emotionally withdrawn
  • Holding space for productive confusion (not rushing to fix it)
  • Anchoring new concepts in the learner's specific world
  • Knowing when the scaffold needs to disappear

Piaget and Vygotsky weren't writing about AI — but their frameworks are sharp enough to tell us exactly where a token predictor falls short. That's the point of theory: it outlasts the technology.
