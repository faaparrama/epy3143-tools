Who belongs in your classroom?

Week 14 instrument · Chapters 4 and 5 · about 4 minutes

The Sentiments, Attitudes, and Concerns about Inclusive Education scale, revised, the SACIE-R (Forlin et al., 2011). It was built for future teachers like you. Fifteen items, three scores. Scored in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, and I never see your answers.

Answer with your first honest reaction. Nobody grades the answer you hoped for.

Inclusive education

The items below are about inclusive education: students from a wide range of backgrounds and abilities learning with their peers in regular schools, where the schools adapt the way they work to meet the needs of all.

1 = strongly disagree · 2 = disagree · 3 = agree · 4 = strongly agree

I am concerned that students with disabilities will not be accepted by the rest of the class.
I dread the thought that I could eventually end up with a disability.
Students who have difficulty expressing their thoughts verbally should be in regular classes.
I am concerned that it will be difficult to give appropriate attention to all students in an inclusive classroom.
I tend to make contacts with people with disabilities brief and I finish them as quickly as possible.
Students who are inattentive should be in regular classes.
I am concerned that my workload will increase if I have students with disabilities in my class.
Students who require communicative technologies (e.g. Braille/sign language) should be in regular classes.
I would feel terrible if I had a disability.
I am concerned that I will be more stressed if I have students with disabilities in my class.
I am afraid to look directly at a person with a disability.
Students who frequently fail exams should be in regular classes.
I find it difficult to overcome my initial shock when meeting people with severe physical disabilities.
I am concerned that I do not have the knowledge and skills required to teach students with disabilities.
Students who need an individualized academic program should be in regular classes.
Comfort (sentiments)
Acceptance (attitudes)
Few concerns (concerns, reversed)
Less comfortable: sentimentsMeeting people with disabilities feels uneasy to you right now. That is common, and it changes with contact: people who spend time with a person with a disability usually score higher here later. Your students will read your comfort before they read your lesson.
Fairly comfortable: sentimentsYou are mostly at ease around people with disabilities, with some discomfort left. Most future teachers land here.
Comfortable: sentimentsDisability doesn't make you uneasy. Use that: you can be the adult in the room who acts normal when a classmate is anxious.
Less accepting: attitudesYou lean toward placing these students outside the regular classroom. Look at which items pulled you down: they name behaviors (failing exams, inattention), and Chapter 5 asks whether the barrier is in the student or in the room.
Moderately accepting: attitudesYou accept some of these students in the regular classroom and hesitate about others. Which ones, and why those?
Accepting: attitudesYou expect these students in your regular classroom. The next question is the one this week asks: once they are there, do you keep the bar?
Many concernsWorkload, stress, attention, your own skills: you have real worries about teaching an inclusive class. Concerns are the score most likely to push a teacher toward "making it easier." Which one is biggest, and what would lower it?
Some concernsYou have some worries about inclusion, at about the level of most future teachers. Name the one that would show up first in your lesson plans.
Few concernsYou feel ready to teach an inclusive class. Check that against experience: confidence before a placement often drops during it, then comes back.

All three bars run from 1 to 4, and higher is more positive toward inclusion. Ten of the items are reverse-scored, as the authors direct, so a high concerns bar means few concerns.

Paste these into Part 1 of your Reflection.

Read these as information about you. A low score is not a verdict on the teacher you will be. These three scores change with contact, training and experience, and the week's question sits right on top of them: a teacher with many concerns is the one most tempted to lower the bar.

Notes

Why this scale, this week. Chapter 5 covers inclusion and the least restrictive environment. The SACIE-R measures three things a teacher brings to that: how comfortable you are around people with disabilities (sentiments), whether you accept learners with different needs in the regular classroom (attitudes), and what worries you about teaching them (concerns).

What this cannot tell you. It is a self-report, before you have taught. It has no national norms, so compare your three bars to each other. Later studies found that a shorter version fits some groups better, so treat the three scores as a starting point for your reflection.
