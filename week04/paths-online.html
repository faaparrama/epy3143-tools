Before you start

This one is about you. Marcia's framework is a lens: nothing here asks you to label yourself.

You decide the level of detail. Part 2 asks what was available to you growing up. You can answer it structurally, without disclosing anything about your family. A general answer, honestly given, is worth full credit.

Part 5 (peer review) isn't on this page. Do it as a Canvas reply this week: post your Part 4, then read one classmate's as a fifteen-year-old who has never met anyone who does that job, and say what that student would still need.

How you got here

A teacher, a parent, a job, a class, a shortage of alternatives. All of these are real answers.

Part 1 — Marcia's two questions

2 pts

Evidence, not labels. Do not name a status.

Name the commitment, then the cost: time, money, family expectation, sunk credits, how it would be received.

Say what made you set it down. If you cannot name one, write that plainly. It is information, and Part 2 is where it gets interesting.

Part 2 — What was actually in the room

2 pts

Exploration needs something to explore. What were the alternatives?

Jobs you had actually seen done, by someone you knew. Most people's list is shorter than they expect.

What would have had to be true for you to see it at fifteen?

Part 3 — Predict, then check an AI

Not graded

Not graded. Does an AI widen what you can see, or hand you back the middle of the distribution?

Prompt 1 — fill in your own three blanks

"I'm a college student. I like ______, I'm good at ______, and people tell me I'm ______. What careers should I consider?"

Prompt 2 — same conversation, straight after

"Now give me ten more that almost nobody would suggest to someone with that description, and say why each one is usually overlooked."

Write your prediction now. An honest "I skipped this" is worth more than a reconstructed guess.

This is the one field that should be pasted.

Then the second list: when you asked for something unusual, was it any good, or just rarer words?

Part 4 — You are about to be someone's only example

2 pts

For some student, you will be the only person they have ever watched do a job that needs your subject. That puts you in their opportunity structure.

Something you could do in a specific lesson, in a specific week, that puts a path in the room. "Expose them to more careers" is too general.

Minutes, coverage, a topic you would otherwise teach. Advice with no cost attached is free to give and free to ignore.
