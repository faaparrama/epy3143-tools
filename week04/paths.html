Before you start

This one is about you, which makes it different from the others. Marcia's framework is a lens, not a verdict — the brief says foreclosure and achievement look identical in a single conversation, and that applies to you too. Nothing here asks you to label yourself.

You decide the level of detail. Part 2 asks what was and wasn't available to you growing up. You can answer that structurally — what a person in that situation could see — without disclosing anything about your family you'd rather not put in writing. A general answer, honestly given, is worth full credit.

Only Part 3's AI box accepts pasted text. Everything else is your own reasoning and has to be typed. If you drafted somewhere else for an access reason, tell Dr. Parra-Martinez and he will take it that way.

How you got here

A teacher, a parent, a job, a class, a shortage of alternatives. All of these are real answers.

Part 1 — Marcia's two questions

2 pts

Evidence, not labels. Do not name a status.

Cost in time, money, family expectation, sunk credits, how it would be received. Be concrete.

Exploration leaves a trail of positions you used to hold. If you cannot name one, write that plainly. It is information, not a failure — and Part 2 is where it gets interesting.

Part 2 — What was actually in the room

2 pts

Exploration requires something to explore. Before anyone calls a commitment premature, it is worth asking what the alternatives were.

Not jobs you'd heard of — jobs you had actually seen done, by someone you knew. Most people's list is shorter than they expect.

And: what would have had to be true for it to be visible to you at fifteen?

Part 3 — Predict → Verify

2 pts

Does an AI widen what you can see, or hand you back the middle of the distribution?

Write your prediction now. The activity does not work backwards, and an honest "I skipped this" is worth more than a reconstructed guess.

Prompt 1 — fill in your own three blanks

"I'm a college student. I like ______, I'm good at ______, and people tell me I'm ______. What careers should I consider?"

Prompt 2 — same conversation, straight after

"Now give me ten more that almost nobody would suggest to someone with that description, and say why each one is usually overlooked."

This is the one field that should be pasted.

Did the first list widen what you could see, or restate it? Did it take an explicit request to get anything unusual — and when it did, was the unusual list any good, or just rarer words?

Part 4 — You are about to be someone's only example

2 pts

For some student in your future classroom, you will be the only person they have ever watched do a job that requires what your subject requires. That makes you part of their opportunity structure whether you intend it or not.

Not "expose them to more careers." Something you could do in a specific lesson, in a specific week, that puts a path in the room that was not there before.

Minutes, coverage, a thing you would otherwise have taught. Advice with no cost attached is free to give and free to ignore.

Part 5 — Peer review

2 pts

Read a classmate's Part 4 as a fifteen-year-old who has never met anyone who does that job. Would their move have reached you?

Use one idea from this week — Marcia, a Big Five trait, or the opportunity-structure argument.
